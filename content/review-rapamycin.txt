== abstract
:Background
Felycin-CA1 (delayed-release sirolimus) is the first drug conditionally approved for feline hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. The approval rests on a single randomised trial of 43 cats.

:Evidence appraised
The full report of the RAPACAT trial, the FDA's corrected Freedom of Information summary for application 141-604 read alongside it, the approved product label, and published event-rate data for subclinical hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

:Findings
The regulatory record and the published report disagree. The FDA states that the sponsor "did not select the effectiveness criteria before the study began" and chose the day-180 endpoint after exploratory analyses; the journal article presents that endpoint as prespecified and does not disclose the sequence. The trial describes itself as nonpivotal, exploratory and dose-determining, and sets alpha at 0.10 on that basis. Maximum wall thickness at day 180 was 7.41 mm on low dose against 8.40 mm on placebo (p = 0.013), with the overall treatment effect not significant (p = 0.150) and three uncorrected contrasts run at the day-180 visit. The endpoint is the largest of four wall measurements taken by two different edge conventions; no individual measurement reached significance at day 180, and the proportion of cats whose wall changed by more than the trial's own 0.5 mm threshold did not differ between groups. The analysis was per protocol, with five of seven withdrawals and all three cardiac withdrawals in the high-dose arm. The two documents also disagree on day-60 significance and on the disposition of the animals.

:Conclusion
A dose-finding study with a post hoc endpoint has been asked to carry a marketing approval, and the fact that the endpoint was post hoc appears only in the regulatory file. Its result is real but fragile, visible only in a composite endpoint and not at the level of the individual cat, and it speaks to a surrogate. The confirmatory trial now under way is powered for that same surrogate and is far too small to establish or exclude a clinical benefit.

== summary
Evidence :: One randomised, placebo-controlled trial in 43 cats, on a surrogate endpoint. No trial has measured heart failure, thromboembolism or survival.
Design :: The report describes itself as nonpivotal, exploratory and dose-determining; the FDA summary calls it a pilot field study. It is the only randomised evidence supporting the approval.
Endpoint :: Change in maximal left ventricular wall thickness — the largest of four measurements taken by two different conventions. The FDA records that it was selected after exploratory analysis; the published report presents it as the prespecified primary endpoint and does not disclose this.
Effect size :: 0.99 mm between low dose and placebo at day 180, p = 0.013. No individual wall measurement reached significance at that visit, and the proportion of cats changing by more than the trial's own 0.5 mm threshold did not differ between groups.
Statistical handling :: Alpha set at 0.10 across three uncorrected contrasts; the overall treatment effect was not significant (p = 0.150); analysed per protocol.
Safety signal :: All three cardiac withdrawals — two heart failure, one sudden death — were in the high-dose arm. None occurred on placebo or low dose. FDA's own view is that the relationship to treatment is unknown.
Hepatic signal :: 15 of 24 cats in the 24-week laboratory safety study had transaminase elevations, against none of eight controls, with no dose-response. Four male littermates reached ALT values of 255 to 4552 U/L against a reference range of 41 to 160.
Pharmacokinetics :: Half-life 58 hours, saturable absorption, non-linear after repeated dosing, accumulating over five months. No interaction study with any CYP3A4 or P-glycoprotein inhibitor.
Regulatory basis :: Conditional approval, on a reasonable-expectation-of-effectiveness standard. The confirmatory trial now running is not constructed to detect an effect on a clinical endpoint.
Not established :: That the drug delays heart failure, prevents thromboembolism or extends survival in cats with subclinical hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

== 1. Background
Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is the most common cardiac disease of cats, affecting roughly 10--15% of the general population, and its subclinical phase has never had a therapy of demonstrated benefit. Rapamycin inhibits mTORC1, a pathway required for pathological cardiac hypertrophy in rodent models, which supplies the rationale for trialling it in this setting.

That rationale cuts in both directions. Complete cardiomyocyte mTOR deletion in mice produces dilated cardiomyopathy and early death, so the strategy depends on a therapeutic window: sufficient inhibition to blunt hypertrophy, insufficient to impair myocyte maintenance. Whether once-weekly dosing reliably falls within that window across a heterogeneous clinical population has not been established. mTOR inhibition additionally impairs insulin signalling; new-onset diabetes occurs in 10--20% of human transplant recipients receiving sirolimus.

On 14 March 2025 the FDA granted conditional approval to Felycin-CA1 (TriviumVet). Conditional approval requires safety together with a "reasonable expectation of effectiveness", a standard set deliberately below that for full approval. It runs one year at a time, renewable for up to four further one-year terms.[2] The label restricts use to cats with left ventricular wall thickness of 6 mm or greater, contraindicates the drug in cats with diabetes mellitus or pre-existing hepatic disease, and warns against co-administration with CYP3A4 or P-glycoprotein inhibitors, calcium channel blockers among them.[3]

The route was not the Minor Use/Minor Species pathway, as is widely assumed. The Freedom of Information summary records that the drug qualified under the 2018 ADUFA IV amendments, which opened conditional approval to drugs that are *not* minor-use or minor-species, and states separately that the product "does not qualify for exclusive marketing rights … because it is not a designated new animal drug".[2] Two criteria had to be met: that the drug treats a serious or life-threatening condition or addresses an unmet need, and that demonstrating effectiveness "would require a complex or particularly difficult study or studies". The FDA's stated grounds for the second were that the disease "makes it unusually time consuming or difficult to enroll sufficient numbers of eligible animals and requires the use of advanced diagnostic tests".[2] That is a claim about feasibility. A 300-cat trial began enrolling within a month of the approval.

== 2. The trial and what it reported
RAPACAT was a multicentre, double-blind, three-arm randomised controlled trial: low-dose delayed-release sirolimus (0.3 mg/kg orally once weekly, n = 15), high-dose (0.6 mg/kg, n = 15), or placebo (n = 13), over 180 days.[1] Fifty-three cats were screened, nine were excluded before enrolment and one after completion, leaving 43 enrolled and 36 with day-180 measurements.

The primary endpoint was prespecified in the methods as change in maximum wall thickness: the greatest of four diastolic measurements, septum and free wall, each obtained by both two-dimensional and M-mode technique. The analysis was a repeated-measures ANCOVA with the screening value as covariate, so the reported least-squares means are adjusted for baseline.

The report describes its own design plainly. Contrasts were evaluated at an unadjusted alpha of 0.10 "due to the nonpivotal, exploratory, and dose-determining nature of this study", and the discussion closes the statistical section by conceding that "the possibility of a type I error must be considered".[1] The regulatory question this appraisal takes up is not whether the trial was competently conducted, but whether a study its own authors classify as exploratory and dose-determining is an adequate basis for marketing a drug.

Both dose arms pooled against placebo gave 7.72 mm, p = 0.054. Across the whole trial the main effect of treatment was not significant (p = 0.150); the treatment-by-visit interaction that licensed the day-180 comparison reached p = 0.084, significant only against the trial's alpha of 0.10.

== 3. Issues with the evidence
A useful discipline drawn from the research-writing literature is not merely to assert that a gap exists but to name its type. Each subsection below is identified accordingly.

== 3. Issues with the evidence
Each of the following is a separate problem with the evidence base, and they compound: the endpoint, the statistical handling, the measurement, the missing animals, the safety record and the regulatory route.

=== 3.1 The published report and the regulatory record disagree about the endpoint
The FDA's corrected Freedom of Information summary states, in its executive summary and again in the effectiveness section: "The sponsor did not select the effectiveness criteria before the study began. After conducting exploratory analyses, the sponsor selected MWT of the LV on Day 180 as the effectiveness endpoint", and "The effectiveness criteria were not selected a priori."[2]

The peer-reviewed report says otherwise. Its methods state that maximum wall thickness change "was assessed as the primary end point", and its discussion defends that endpoint as "chosen on the basis of the known pathophysiology of HCM".[1] Nowhere does it disclose that the endpoint was selected after the data had been examined. A reader of the journal article alone cannot know this; only the regulatory file records it.

The two documents diverge in two further places. The summary reports the low-dose difference as statistically significant "on Day 60 and Day 180", where the published Table 2 gives p = 0.564 at day 60. The summary also gives within-arm changes at day 180 that do not appear in the paper at all: a fall of 0.17 mm on low dose, rises of 0.94 mm on placebo and 0.50 mm on high dose.

Post hoc endpoint selection is among the strongest known generators of false-positive findings, and it compounds every statistical concern set out below. Its non-disclosure in the published report is a separate problem, and the more serious one: the journal record, which is what practitioners read, is missing the single fact that most affects how the result should be weighed.

=== 3.2 An exploratory dose-finding study is carrying a marketing approval
The trial's stated purpose was to find a dose. Its authors describe it three times as nonpivotal, exploratory and dose-determining, and set the alpha threshold accordingly; the FDA summary calls it a "pilot field study" throughout.[1,2] Studies of that kind are meant to generate the hypothesis a pivotal trial then tests; they are not designed to establish that a drug works, and their statistical thresholds are chosen on the understanding that a confirmatory trial follows.

What followed instead was conditional approval. No pivotal trial had reported at the time of that decision, and the report of the exploratory study is the only randomised evidence in the public record. The trial is not at fault. The distance between what it was built to answer and what it is now asked to support is. The report's own conclusion asks for less than the approval grants: that delayed-release rapamycin is "a promising novel drug for continued study".[1]

The rest of what the approval rests on is thin in a way the summary does not conceal. Alongside the trial, the FDA lists three published papers: two mouse aortic-banding experiments[6,7] and one study of 70 human cardiac transplant recipients switched from a calcineurin inhibitor to sirolimus.[8] The mouse work establishes that mTOR inhibition attenuates pressure-overload hypertrophy, which is the mechanistic rationale and not evidence in cats. The transplant study is confounded by design: the hypertrophic stimulus, the calcineurin inhibitor, is being withdrawn at the same moment sirolimus is introduced, so the regression it reports cannot be attributed to the drug that replaced it.

=== 3.3 A relaxed alpha, three uncorrected contrasts, and a null overall test
Alpha was set at 0.10, twice the conventional threshold, and the day-180 comparisons were run unadjusted: low dose against placebo (p = 0.013), high dose against placebo (p = 0.36), and both doses pooled against placebo (p = 0.054).[1] Three overlapping contrasts at a lenient threshold, with no correction, is a permissive statistical environment by any standard.

The comparisons were licensed by a treatment-by-visit interaction of p = 0.084, which is significant only because alpha was 0.10; at the conventional threshold the analysis would have stopped at the main effect of treatment, which was p = 0.150. The chain from a null overall test through a marginal interaction to a significant within-visit contrast is not a chain the exploratory design was powered to support, and the authors say as much.

=== 3.4 The signal exists in the composite and nowhere else
The difference at day 180 is 0.99 mm, twice the 0.5 mm the trial's own power calculation nominated as detectable beyond biological variability.[1] The effect is not trivially small. The difficulty lies elsewhere.

The FDA summary reports the same result differently, as within-arm change rather than between-arm difference: a fall of 0.17 mm on low dose against rises of 0.94 mm on placebo and 0.50 mm on high dose.[2] On those figures the high-dose arm, which received twice the drug, progressed at half the placebo rate and more than the low-dose arm, with no monotonic relationship between dose and effect. In a study whose declared purpose was to determine a dose, that is the finding that most needs explaining, and neither document explains it.

The endpoint takes the largest of four measurements, and the four do not share a convention: two-dimensional septal thickness was measured leading edge to trailing edge, two-dimensional free wall leading edge to leading edge, and both M-mode measurements leading edge to leading edge. Taking a maximum across measurements of differing bias is upward-biased by construction and sensitive to whichever measurement happens to run high on the day.

Examined individually, no wall measurement carried the result. Septal thickness by two-dimensional imaging, septal thickness by M-mode and free-wall thickness by M-mode all showed no treatment effect (p = 0.773, 0.779 and 0.663). Only two-dimensional free-wall thickness differed from placebo, and only when the two visits were pooled (p = 0.032).[1]

Nor did the effect appear at the level of the individual animal. The trial defined a change worth detecting as 0.5 mm. The proportion of cats whose wall thickness moved by more than that from screening did not differ between groups. A clinician cannot therefore identify a responding cat, and the group-level difference is generated as much by movement in the placebo arm, from 7.52 mm at day 60 to 8.40 mm at day 180, as by stability in the treated one.

=== 3.5 No intention-to-treat analysis
Thirty-six of 43 randomised cats were measured at day 180 and the analysis was confined to them. Five of the seven withdrawals were in the high-dose arm, and three of those five left because they developed congestive heart failure or died.[1] Removing the animals with the worst outcomes from the arm in which they occurred works in the drug's favour, and it did not suffice to produce a high-dose benefit. Whether it distorted the low-dose result cannot be established from the published data: this is missing outcome data that may well be informative, and the standard remedies have not been published: an intention-to-treat analysis, or a sensitivity analysis under plausible assumptions about the missing cats. The two documents do not even agree on the disposition: the FDA summary records six exclusions and 37 cats still enrolled at day 180, the paper seven withdrawals and 36 completing.[1,2]

The report states that echocardiographic values did not differ significantly between groups at baseline, and the model adjusted for the screening value, so the wall-thickness comparison is baseline-adjusted. Body weight was not: mean weights were 6.06 kg in placebo, 5.97 kg in the low-dose arm and 4.94 kg in the high-dose arm.[1] Wall thickness scales with body size, and an unadjusted difference of roughly a kilogram between arms in a trial of 43 cats is worth reporting alongside the result.

=== 3.6 Every serious cardiac event occurred in the high-dose arm
Congestive heart failure, congestive heart failure ending in euthanasia, and death following seizures: three of fifteen high-dose cats, against none on placebo and none on low dose.[1] Four of the trial's five serious adverse events were in cats receiving rapamycin. Total adverse-event counts did not differ between groups (21, 23 and 25 for placebo, low and high dose; p = 0.82), so the signal is in the severity and its distribution rather than in the overall burden.

Attributing that distribution to chance requires assuming randomisation concentrated the sickest animals in one arm. Excess mTOR inhibition impairing myocyte autophagy is a mechanistically plausible alternative that the published record does not evaluate. The trial is far too small to distinguish between them, which is precisely the point: the dose selected for marketing was selected in a study that could not have detected harm at the dose it rejected. The report concludes that the drug "was well-tolerated at both doses studied";[1] that conclusion follows from the absence of a significant difference in adverse-event counts, and a trial of fifteen cats per arm cannot support it.

The FDA reached the same impasse from the other side. Its summary records that the three cats "all had risk factors associated with progression of HCM at enrollment", two with severe pre-existing structural disease and one with an NT-proBNP of 1344 pmol/L. It then states that "it is unknown if the heart failure and sudden death were associated with treatment".[2] That is the correct conclusion, and it is not the same as the paper's, which reports the drug as well tolerated at both doses.

The low-dose arm's death from diabetic ketoacidosis is a recognised sirolimus class effect, and the FDA summary is more specific than the paper about how it happened: the cat developed diabetes during the study, "treatment for diabetes was not initiated, and the cat continued on the study", before presenting in ketoacidosis and dying.[2] Baseline glucose and liver enzymes, with ongoing monitoring, should be treated as routine.

=== 3.7 The liver signal is larger than the label conveys
Three laboratory safety studies were submitted. In the 24-week margin of safety study, **15 of the 24 treated cats** had at least one transaminase elevation and none of the eight controls did. The elevations appeared in all three dose groups and, in the FDA's words, "a dose-response was not present".[2] Four male littermates had the most severe values: maximum AST of 110 to 515 U/L against a reference range of 16 to 34, and maximum **ALT of 255 to 4552 U/L** against a reference range of 41 to 160. In three of the four the elevation was present at the first post-treatment sample, on day 26 or 27, and persisted throughout the 24 weeks.

All four cats remained clinically normal and post-mortem examination found no liver pathology, which is the basis on which the study concluded the drug was well tolerated. Two things sit uncomfortably with that conclusion. An ALT of 4552 U/L is roughly twenty-eight times the upper limit of normal and is not a laboratory curiosity in any other context. And the concentration of the severe cases in four littermates points at heritable susceptibility, which no study has followed up and no test can currently identify in a client-owned cat.

The shorter dose-tolerance study found milder elevations, up to twice the upper limit of normal, in 8 of 24 cats, and these resolved within four weeks of stopping. That is reassuring about reversibility and it is why the margin of safety study, which had no recovery period, cannot speak to it.

The one death in the safety record belongs to a cat that was not in these studies at all. In a pilot field study in chronic kidney disease, a cat enrolled with pre-existing liver disease and a raised alkaline phosphatase developed progressive inappetence, rising ALP, ALT and AST, and icterus, and was euthanised about four months after leaving the study.[2] The label's contraindication in pre-existing hepatic disease rests on that single animal.

=== 3.8 Non-linear, accumulating, and never studied alongside the drugs it will meet
The pharmacokinetics are more awkward than weekly dosing suggests. At 1.3 times the label dose the half-life was 58.3 hours, with a 95% confidence interval running from 34 to 99 hours. Dose-normalised peak concentrations *fell* as dose rose, which the FDA reads as absorption becoming saturated at higher doses, and comparison of exposure at day 0 with day 147 "suggests that the pharmacokinetics are non-linear after multiple dosing", with accumulation ratios of 1.33 for peak concentration and 1.62 for exposure.[2]

Three consequences follow, none of them addressed anywhere in the record. Exposure at six months is not the exposure at week one, so a safety profile established over 24 weeks of dosing may not describe a cat treated for years. Saturable absorption means the relationship between the 0.3 and 0.6 mg/kg arms of the trial was not a simple doubling, which bears directly on why the higher dose fared worse and was never analysed. And a drug that accumulates non-linearly is the kind whose exposure a metabolic inhibitor is most likely to push somewhere unexpected. That is what the label's warning against CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein inhibitors anticipates, with no feline interaction study behind it.[3]

=== 3.9 The safety studies did not enrol the patients, the dose, or the individual cat
The three laboratory studies used healthy purpose-bred cats: 32 aged 10 to 11 months, 32 aged 2 to 5 years, and 20 aged about 4 months.[2] None had hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. The treated population is middle-aged and older cats with cardiac disease, frequently alongside chronic kidney disease or hyperthyroidism, and the safety record does not describe them.

Nor was the label regimen itself tested. The lowest dose in the 24-week study was 1.3 times the label dose; the dose-tolerance study gave the drug three times weekly rather than once. Neither GLP study administered 0.3 mg/kg once weekly.

The analysis units compound this. In the margin of safety study "the experimental unit was the pair housed room", and for binary outcomes an event in one or both cats counted as a single event for the room; continuous variables were averaged across the two animals. Sixteen rooms is a smaller study than 32 cats, and averaging conceals precisely the individual outlier that the four littermates turned out to be. The analysis was descriptive throughout, with no hypothesis testing.

The vaccine response study has the same shape of limitation. Its endpoint was that every treated cat reach a rabies titre above 0.5 IU/mL, which all twelve did.[2] That is a threshold, not a comparison: a drug could blunt titres substantially and still pass. The study addressed one killed vaccine in vaccine-naive kittens over 56 days, and says nothing about infection risk in older cats on an mTOR inhibitor for years.

=== 3.10 The confirmatory trial is not designed to answer the question that matters
The approximately 300-cat HALT trial began dosing in April 2025 across sites in the United States, United Kingdom and Europe; the 12-month dosing phase runs into late 2026, with results expected in 2027. It is powered for the echocardiographic surrogate, not for clinical outcomes.

At the 2--6% annual event rates observed in subclinical hypertrophic cardiomyopathy cohorts,[4,5] 300 cats followed for a year would accrue roughly 6 to 18 congestive heart failure events in total. Detecting even a halving of risk against a 4% control rate requires on the order of 800 cats per arm. A trial of this size can confirm the surrogate; it cannot rule a clinical benefit in or out.

=== 3.11 Nothing in the public record requires an outcome study
The Freedom of Information summary sets out what conditional approval is and how long it lasts, one year renewable for up to four further one-year terms. It says nothing about what evidence full approval will require, nor whether any post-approval study is mandatory.[2] Unlike the FDA's human accelerated-approval framework, no obligation to measure a clinical outcome has been disclosed. On the public record, two studies confirming the same echocardiographic surrogate would suffice. The eligibility criterion that admitted the drug to this pathway was that an effectiveness study would be unusually difficult to conduct; nothing requires that the difficulty be revisited once it has been shown to be surmountable. Full approval, if granted on that basis, should be read as *surrogate confirmed twice*, not as *clinical benefit demonstrated*.

== 4. What would close the gap
A pivotal trial with a registered protocol and a genuinely prespecified endpoint, adequately powered at a conventional alpha, reporting a single wall measurement by a single convention alongside the composite. A published intention-to-treat analysis of RAPACAT. A responder analysis that separates treated from untreated cats, which the present one does not. Independent replication by investigators without a commercialisation interest. A feline interaction study covering the CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein inhibitors the label warns against. And a clinical outcomes trial measuring congestive heart failure, arterial thromboembolism and survival, imposed as a condition of full approval rather than left to follow it.

== 5. Conclusion
RAPACAT found what it was built to look for: a dose-ranging signal on an echocardiographic measure. The low-dose difference is of a size that matters if it is real. Whether it is real rests on an endpoint chosen after the data were examined, a relaxed alpha, three uncorrected contrasts descending from a null overall test, a composite that mixes measurement conventions, and a per-protocol analysis that removed the animals that fared worst from the arm in which they fared worst. No individual wall measurement and no per-animal responder analysis supports it. The safety distribution in the high-dose arm is unexplained and the trial was never large enough to explain it.

Conditional approval is not equivalent to demonstrated clinical benefit, and full approval, if granted on the present trajectory, would confirm a surrogate rather than an outcome.

One further point belongs to the literature rather than to the drug. The single fact that most changes how this result should be read, that the endpoint was selected after exploratory analysis, is in the regulatory file and not in the journal. Where a licensed product is concerned, the peer-reviewed report is not the complete record, and reading it as though it were will give the wrong answer.

The appropriate reading is that a plausible mechanism has been tested once, in a study designed to choose a dose rather than to prove a benefit, and that the question of whether the drug helps cats remains open.
