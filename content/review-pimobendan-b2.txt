== abstract
:Claim
That pimobendan delays the onset of congestive heart failure
in dogs with stage B2 preclinical mitral valve disease. One randomised trial supports it, and it is
the best trial in the field.

:Method
The published survival curves were digitised and
approximate individual-level event times reconstructed, reproducing the paper's medians, hazard ratio and P value, so
that analyses the trial does not report could be run.

:Robustness
Nine reclassified dogs remove statistical
significance. On proportions alone the result is not significant (P = 0.135). The entire absolute
separation between the arms occurs in the first six months, and significance is lost once 23 weeks
are removed, faster than the loss of events can explain.

:Residual effect
A real effect. Restarting the clock at 23 weeks still
leaves a 380-day median difference and 107 days of average event-free time gained, so this is not
only early failures.

:Regulatory basis
Three non-identical versions of the trial are on
the public record and none cross-references the others. FDA judged the trial not adequate and
well-controlled without saying why. Full approval rests on a single-arm study whose success
threshold was set below its own historical control rate.

:Position
Treat dogs that meet the trial's criteria. Whether dogs
at the mild end of stage B2 benefit is not answerable from the published data, and the analysis that
would answer several of these questions has already been collected and not reported.

== summary
Evidence :: One randomised, placebo-controlled, blinded, multicentre trial. 360 dogs; 354 in the analysis of the primary endpoint (178 pimobendan, 176 control).[1]
Entry criteria :: LA/Ao &ge; 1.6, LVIDDN &ge; 1.7 and VHS &gt; 10.5, all three required. These are also the numeric criteria on the current label.[1,7]
Endpoint :: Composite: onset of congestive heart failure, cardiac death, or euthanasia for cardiac disease. Roughly one dog in six reached it by dying rather than by developing heart failure.
Reported effect :: Median time to endpoint 1228 days against 766; hazard ratio 0.64 (95% CI 0.47--0.87); log-rank P = .0038. Median survival 1059 days against 902 (P = .012).
Robustness :: Reclassifying **nine** late placebo events as censored removes significance. On proportions alone, 74 of 178 against 88 of 176, Fisher's exact P = 0.135.
Where the effect sits :: By day 180 the arms differ by 16 events, more than the 14 they finish with; thereafter they accrue 63 and 61. Removing 23 weeks gives P = 0.0501, against 0.0075 expected had the effect been constant.
What survives that cut :: A 380-day median difference and 107 days of mean event-free time gained, in dogs alive and event-free at six months. The benefit is not confined to early failures.
Regulatory basis :: Full approval (NADA 141-273, 19 December 2025) rests on a single-arm study of 125 dogs against a historical control. EPIC was treated as corroborative only, having "not met certain characteristics of an adequate and well-controlled study."[5]
Not established :: Whether dogs at the mild end of stage B2 benefit; whether survival after heart failure is shortened in dogs treated beforehand. EPIC holds randomised data on the second question that have never been analysed.

== 1. What the trial reported
EPIC randomised dogs with preclinical myxomatous mitral valve disease and cardiomegaly to
pimobendan or placebo, and reported a median time to the primary endpoint of 1228 days against
766, a hazard ratio of 0.64 and a log-rank P of .0038.[1] The primary endpoint was a composite:
the onset of congestive heart failure, cardiac death, or euthanasia for cardiac disease.

It is the best-conducted trial in canine cardiology and the basis on which pimobendan is now
licensed for stage B2 disease. This appraisal takes the result seriously enough to ask how much of
it survives being pulled apart, and then sets it beside what the regulatory record actually says.

== 2. Rebuilding the trial
The published survival curves were digitised and approximate individual-level event times
reconstructed from them, so that analyses the paper does not report could be run. These are
pseudo-individual event times recovered from the curve, not the trial's own patient records;
nothing here recovers an actual dog. Group sizes and total event counts were fixed to the published
values; medians, hazard ratio and P were left free, so agreement with the paper is a genuine check
rather than an assumption.

| Primary endpoint | Reconstructed | Published[1] |
|---|---|---|
| Median, pimobendan | 1225 d | 1228 d |
| Median, placebo | 758 d | 766 d |
| Hazard ratio | 0.630 | 0.64 |
| Log-rank P | 0.0039 | 0.0038 |

The same procedure on the all-cause mortality curve gives medians of 1058 and 901 days against a
published 1059 and 902, and a hazard ratio of 0.652, which the paper does not report.

**Read this section with the reconstruction in mind.** Everything that follows
in sections 3 to 7, and the modelling in section 14, is computed on reconstructed data, not on the
trial's own dataset. Reconstruction from published curves recovers group-level quantities well
and individual dogs only approximately. The agreement above is a reason to take these analyses
seriously; it is not a substitute for the investigators running them.

== 3. When the separation happens
By day 180 the placebo arm has accrued 27 events against 11 on pimobendan. That gap of 16 events
is already larger than the 14-event difference the trial finishes with. Over the whole of the
remaining follow-up the two arms accrue 63 and 61 events respectively.

Put plainly: the entire absolute separation between the arms occurs in the first six months, and
after that the two curves fall at the same rate.

== 4. Dropping the early period
If the effect were confined to that early period, removing it should dissolve the result.
Removing the first several weeks from both arms and restarting the clock:

| Weeks removed | Events left | Hazard ratio | 95% CI | Observed P | Expected P |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 162 | 0.630 | 0.46--0.86 | 0.0039 | 0.0033 |
| 8 | 158 | 0.639 | 0.46--0.88 | 0.0056 | 0.0037 |
| 16 | 150 | 0.640 | 0.46--0.89 | 0.0072 | 0.0047 |
| 23 | 134 | 0.709 | 0.50--1.00 | 0.0501 | 0.0075 |
| 26 | 124 | 0.715 | 0.50--1.03 | 0.0664 | 0.0101 |
| 39 | 111 | 0.732 | 0.50--1.07 | 0.1061 | 0.0149 |
| 52 | 91 | 0.765 | 0.50--1.17 | 0.2109 | 0.0275 |

Significance goes at 23 weeks, with 134 of the original 162 events still in hand.

On its own that is only a power argument, and it deserves to be dismissed as one: a quarter of
the events have gone and the point estimate has moved no further than 0.63 to 0.71. The argument
only works as a comparison. The last column is the P that would be expected if the hazard ratio
never changed and the only thing lost were events, taken from the standard approximation
SE(log HR) = 2/&radic;<span class="rt">d</span>. At 134 events a constant effect of 0.630 would
still give P = 0.0075. What is observed is P = 0.0501, nearly seven times higher.

[[figure: review-pimobendan-b2-fig1.html]]

The observed P deteriorates faster than the loss of events can account for. That divergence is
the argument, and the bare loss of significance is not.

== 5. What survives the cut
Losing significance is not the same as losing the effect. Restarting the clock at 23 weeks and
asking what is left:

| Analysis | Median, pimobendan | Median, placebo | Difference | Mean event-free time gained |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Whole trial | 1225 d | 758 d | 466 d | 139 d |
| From day 161 (23 weeks) | 1064 d | 684 d | 380 d | 107 d |

Restricted mean survival is calculated to 1200 days and does not depend on where the curves cross
fifty per cent. In calendar terms the day-161 analysis puts the medians at 1225 and 845 days from
randomisation.

So conditioning on a dog being alive and event-free at six months, the median gap is still 380
days and the average event-free time gained is still 107. Smaller than the headline, and a long way
from nothing. If the whole result were early failures, the residual gap among dogs nowhere near
heart failure at six months should be small. It is not.

== 6. How fragile the result is
Taking placebo events latest-first and reclassifying them as censored, which is the least
favourable realistic change that could be made. This is a sensitivity analysis, not the
conventional fragility index: the fragility index converts events to non-events in the smaller
arm of a two-by-two table, whereas this reclassifies late events as censorings in a
time-to-event analysis. The counts below are therefore not comparable to published fragility
indices and are not offered as such.

| Analysis | Events | Starting P | Dogs needed to lose P &lt; 0.05 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole trial | 162 | 0.0039 | 9 |
| From day 56 | 158 | 0.0056 | 8 |
| From day 112 | 150 | 0.0072 | 7 |
| From day 161 | 134 | 0.0501 | already above 0.05 |

Nine dogs. Reclassify nine late placebo events as censored and the trial loses significance. For
scale, the journal and FDA accounts of this same trial differ from each other by more than that:
the control arm gains three events between the two, and loses six dogs.

== 7. Timing, not counts
Judged on proportions alone, 74 of 178 against 88 of 176, Fisher's exact test
gives P = 0.135. Not significant.

All of EPIC's statistical significance comes from *when* dogs had events, not from
how many did. Much the same number of dogs reached heart failure in each arm. They reached it at
different times.

That is not a criticism. Delaying the onset of heart failure is precisely what the drug claims to
do, and a time-to-event analysis is the right way to test it. But it explains why the result is so
sensitive to any analysis that touches timing, and why nine reclassifications can undo it.

== 8. Three versions on the public record
The 2022 conditional approval and the 2025 full approval both summarise this trial. Neither
agrees with the published paper, and they do not agree with each other.

The 2025 summary prints three population figures and they should not be
conflated: 363 dogs enrolled, an effectiveness population of 353, and 343
"included in the effectiveness population in the final analysis" (173 pimobendan and
170 control), which is the denominator for the event counts above.[5]

The 2022 version sits almost exactly on the paper, down to the component event counts. The 2025
version has ten fewer dogs and a stronger effect. The control arm loses six dogs and gains three
events, which is what drags the hazard ratio down.

There is a plausible and innocent explanation, and the documents supply it. The paper describes an
endpoint committee reviewing the radiographs and the case records.[1] The 2025 summary states
that "each case of CHF was verified by an endpoint committee, under masked conditions, based
on the radiographs alone."[5] Re-adjudicating the same dogs under a narrower rule would
move cases in both directions, which is what the component counts show.

That can be tested. On the reconstructed data, removing five and six dogs from the two arms and
reclassifying a few early censorings as events reproduces the FDA hazard ratio at 0.596 against
their 0.591, the control median at 727 against 732, and the P at 0.0011 against 0.0008. So this is
one dataset under two adjudication rules, not two different trials.

What is not resolved is that nothing cross-references the three. Anyone citing EPIC is citing one
version of it without being told the others exist.

== 9. What the regulator did
The 2025 summary states that EPIC "did not meet certain characteristics of an adequate and
well-controlled study [21 CFR 514.117]," and that it was "considered corroborative to
support this indication when evaluated in conjunction with Study No. 2019035, a single-arm field
study with a historical control."[5]

It never says which characteristics. The regulation is cited; the deficiencies are not
enumerated anywhere in the document.

The 2022 conditional approval was softer, accepting the data as a reasonable expectation of
effectiveness.[4] Pimobendan was therefore marketed for preclinical B2 disease from June 2022
under conditional approval, on a reasonable-expectation standard, for three and a half years before
the full approval of 19 December 2025.[4,5]

== 10. The pivotal study, and what it was measured against
The study that converted conditional approval into full approval was 2019035: in FDA's own
words a "historically controlled, open-label, multi-center field study," single-arm,
with 125 dogs in the effectiveness population and one year of follow-up.[5]

It reported a treatment success rate of "79.2% (95% confidence interval 70.3 to
85.9%)." The criteria for success were fixed in advance: "the lower bound of the 95%
confidence interval was greater than or equal to 54% and the estimated success rate for pimobendan
was greater than or equal to 65%."[5] Nothing in the document explains where either
threshold came from.

The historical comparator was EPIC. The summary states that "the historical control was
derived from a subset of the control population enrolled in the EPIC study … that met the
enrollment criteria for this study," and, separately, that applying the new success criteria
to EPIC "cases enrolled at US sites … resulted in a success rate of 80% with a lower
80% confidence interval of 72%."[5]

Two things follow, and the first is the more important.

**The success threshold was set below the comparator.** If the 80 per cent figure
describes untreated EPIC dogs, then the bar the pivotal study had to clear, a point estimate
of 65 per cent, sat fifteen points beneath the historical control rate, and the observed 79.2
per cent did not exceed that rate but matched it. The study was never required to beat its own
comparator, and the document records no comparison against it. A single-arm design is not worthless in itself. The difficulty is that without a contemporaneous
randomised comparator the
result depends entirely on whether the historical benchmark describes a population comparable to the
one enrolled, and the benchmark here was set below the rate it was drawn from.

**The derivation of the benchmark is under-specified.** The two passages describe
the source differently: a subset of the control population meeting this study's
enrolment criteria in one place, and cases enrolled at US sites in another. They are not stated to
be the same dogs. The arithmetic adds to the doubt: the failure definition in 2019035 is broader
than EPIC's endpoint, so applying it to EPIC must give a *lower* rate than the
event-free rate. EPIC placebo dogs at LA/Ao 1.8 and above are in the region of 70 per cent
event-free at one year on the narrow definition, which does not obviously leave room for 80 per cent
on a broader one. That estimate scales the published hazard ratio for LA/Ao by the study's own
failure breakdown; it is arithmetic on published numbers rather than a measurement, and it is
offered as a reason the derivation should be stated rather than as a finding.

Two further features of the design deserve recording. Dogs with "current or previous
evidence of clinically significant coughing" or "current or previous evidence of
syncope" were excluded; new syncope, and "advanced coughing that required concomitant
therapy in absence of radiographic CHF," then counted as treatment failures.[5] And entry
required LA/Ao &ge; 1.8 and VHS &gt; 10.5, with no LVIDDN criterion at all,[5] so the two studies
did not define stage B2 the same way.

== 11. The label
The approved indication is qualitative: asymptomatic disease with "a moderate or loud mitral
murmur due to mitral regurgitation and cardiomegaly."[7] The numbers sit further down, under
Precautions, where stage B2 cardiomegaly is defined as meeting *all three* of VHS &gt; 10.5,
LA/Ao &ge; 1.6 and LVIDDN &ge; 1.7.[7] Two of the three are echocardiographic, so a dog cannot
meet the label's definition on a radiograph alone.

Those three values are EPIC's enrolment criteria.[1] They are not the pivotal
study's, which required LA/Ao &ge; 1.8 and set no LVIDDN threshold.[5]

So the label's numeric gate is drawn from the trial the regulator declined to treat as
adequate and well-controlled, rather than from the study that supported full approval. Dogs with an
LA/Ao between 1.6 and 1.8 are label-eligible and were never enrolled in 2019035. For that group the
entire evidence base is EPIC.

One line has changed wording between documents, and it is worth recording because it is the only
thing standing between the indication and a dog without cardiomegaly. The 2022 conditional summary
said that the drug "should also not be administered to dogs before they develop cardiomegaly,
as this causes an increased risk of cardiac pathology associated with exaggerated hemodynamic
responses."[4] That sentence does not appear in the 2025 summary. The current label carries
the substance in different words: "If therapy is initiated prior to the development of
cardiomegaly, treated dogs are at risk for cardiac pathology associated with exaggerated hemodynamic
responses to VETMEDIN."[7] The warning survives; only its phrasing has moved.

Its origin is the original 2007 safety package. In healthy Beagles at three and five times the
label dose, pimobendan produced "severe left ventricular hypertrophy with multifocal
subendocardial ischemic lesions," "moderate to marked myxomatous thickening of the
mitral valves," and new murmurs of mitral insufficiency.[6] At the label dose there was
nothing beyond what control dogs showed. This is not evidence of harm at 0.5 mg/kg. It is the
reason the contraindication exists.

== 12. The randomised test EPIC already contains
Two retrospective cohorts report shorter survival after heart failure in dogs treated
beforehand.[8,9] Both are confounded by indication, and the appraisal of that question is
<a href="gapmap.html#q-does-preclinical-pimobendan-shorten-survival-once-heart-fa">on the gap map
separately</a>.

What appears to have been missed is that EPIC contains a randomised version of the same question.
Figure 1 of the trial reports, for dogs that reached the primary endpoint, what happened to them
afterwards:[1]

| Arm | Reached endpoint | Later died | Alive at close | Lost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pimobendan | 74 | 54 (73.0%) | 17 | 3 |
| Placebo | 88 | 69 (78.4%) | 16 | 3 |

The only post-endpoint outcome data EPIC publishes.

This is a randomised comparison of survival after heart failure, in the same dogs, with allocation
by chance rather than by clinician judgement. It has never been published as a time-to-event
analysis. The crude proportions cannot settle it: Fisher's exact P is 0.464, and pimobendan
dogs reached the endpoint later, so they had less remaining follow-up before the study closed.

Simulating the analysis with EPIC's own event counts and censoring structure, at the hazard
ratio the retrospective work reports the trial would detect it about 91 per cent of the time. At a
modest effect of 1.3 it would not. So this is a specific, adequately powered analysis on data
already collected, free of the confounding that undermines the retrospective studies, and reported
so far only as counts in a flow diagram.

== 13. Selection or harm
There is an account of the retrospective findings that requires no harm from the drug at all.

A dog that reaches heart failure despite being on pimobendan is, by definition, a dog in which the
drug did not do what it was given to do. Treated dogs arriving in heart failure are therefore a
selected group of non-responders. Untreated dogs arriving there are an unselected mixture of
animals, some of whom would have responded had they been treated. Give both groups pimobendan at
decompensation and the pretreated group does worse, because it has been enriched for animals the
drug was never going to help.

That is a selection effect, and it would produce exactly the pattern the retrospective studies
report. It also explains the early concentration of benefit at the same time, since dogs closer to
decompensation supply both most of the events and most of the opportunity to delay them.

Selection and harm make opposite predictions about one thing, which is what makes them
separable:

- <h3>If the drug causes harm</h3>

The deficit should grow with exposure. More
time on treatment, worse outcome afterwards.

- <h3>If it is selection</h3>

The deficit should be largest in dogs that reached
heart failure quickly despite treatment, since those are the most refractory, and should shrink in
dogs that stayed preclinical for years before failing.

The test is post-heart-failure survival regressed on time to heart failure, within the treated
arm. A positive slope favours selection; a negative slope favours harm. The retrospective studies
cannot do this, because the duration of preclinical treatment was not recorded. EPIC has it exactly:
time to the primary endpoint is the trial's own primary variable.

== 14. Does the trade-off still favour treating?
If pimobendan delays heart failure but dogs then survive a shorter time after it, the question is
whether the strategy still comes out ahead on total time. That can be modelled by adding a
preclinical phase and a post-heart-failure phase, taking the first from EPIC and the second from the
retrospective studies.[8,9]

One correction is needed first. The EPIC primary endpoint is a composite, not the date of heart
failure; roughly one dog in six reached it by dying of a cardiac cause and has no post-heart-failure
phase at all. Adding one inflates every strategy, so the post-CHF term below is scaled by 0.833.

| Strategy | Preclinical | Post-CHF | Total | vs reference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No preclinical pimobendan | 840 d | 382 d | 1222 d | --- |
| Pimobendan in B2 | 1031 d | 179 d | 1209 d | −13 d |
| Wait until CHF, then pimobendan | 840 d | 284 d | 1124 d | −98 d |

On that basis treating in B2 is roughly break-even once the reported post-heart-failure penalty is
carried, and waiting until heart failure is clearly the worst of the three.

But the answer turns on one choice. The preclinical advantage can be measured two ways and they
disagree badly. The area between the observed curves out to day 1570 gives 190 days. The mean of a
Weibull fitted to the same data and extrapolated to infinity gives 478. Neither is right: the first
truncates the pimobendan curve while it is still at 40 per cent and understates; the second
extrapolates far beyond any data and overstates. On the measured basis treating in B2 loses 13 days.
On the extrapolated basis it gains 275. **The comparison changes sign inside a range the trial
never observed.**

Break-even sits at a post-heart-failure median penalty of about 179 days on the measured basis.
The reported penalties are 191 days[8] and 269 days.[9] So on measured data the reported
penalties just cancel the preclinical gain, and on extrapolated data they do not come close.

The one result stable across both bases is that waiting until heart failure before starting
pimobendan is the worst of the three strategies, by roughly 98 days.

This is a way of framing the trade-off, not an estimate. It combines three studies
with different populations and endpoints, and both post-heart-failure inputs are retrospective. The
analysis in section 12 would replace both of them with a randomised comparison and settle which side
of break-even this falls on.

== 15. Where this leaves it
The randomised trial provides evidence of a treatment effect, and the effect is less secure than
the headline estimate suggests. Reclassifying nine dogs removes statistical significance. The
proportions alone are not significant. The great majority of the absolute separation happens in the
first six months.

It is not confined to that period. Restarting the clock at 23 weeks still leaves a 380-day median
difference and 107 days of average event-free time gained, so dogs nowhere near heart failure at six
months still show a benefit. That landmark comparison is computed on reconstructed curves and is not
a randomised landmark analysis of patient-level data; it cannot establish the effect with the
certainty the trial's own data would.

The most defensible reading is that dogs approaching heart failure have the most to gain, because
they have the most events available to delay, and that the trial's statistical signal is
dominated by them. Whether dogs at the mild end of B2 benefit is not answerable from the published
data. EPIC found no significant treatment-by-severity interaction, but it was never powered to find
one, and no detected interaction is not evidence that none exists.

The regulatory record is the sharper problem. The trial was judged not adequate and
well-controlled without a stated reason; the pivotal evidence is a single-arm study with no
concurrent comparator and success thresholds of unexplained origin, set below the historical control
rate; and the label's numeric criteria come from the trial the regulator set aside rather than
from the study that replaced it.

**What would settle it.** From the investigators: time from the primary endpoint to
death, by randomised arm, as Kaplan--Meier curves and a Cox model; within the pimobendan arm,
post-endpoint survival regressed on time to endpoint, whose slope distinguishes selection from harm;
and the baseline and day-35 characteristics of dogs reaching the endpoint, by arm, day 35 being where
the trial's own longitudinal analysis finds change in heart size becomes prognostic.[2,3]
From FDA, four questions each answerable in a sentence: which characteristics of an adequate and
well-controlled study EPIC failed; whether the 80 per cent historical figure was computed on control
dogs or treated dogs; how many dogs were in that historical group; and where the 54 per cent
threshold came from.
